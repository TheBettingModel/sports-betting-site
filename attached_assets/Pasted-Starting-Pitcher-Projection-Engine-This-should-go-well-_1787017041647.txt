Starting Pitcher Projection Engine — This should go well beyond ERA. You want xERA/FIP/xFIP/SIERA-style estimators, K%, BB%, K-BB%, swinging-strike/whiff rate, velocity trends, pitch mix, platoon splits, times-through-order effects, recent workload, and expected innings. The key is projecting how the pitcher should perform today, not describing what he did historically.
Bullpen Strength + Availability — A huge percentage of MLB bets are decided after the starter leaves. Grade bullpen quality, leverage relievers, handedness, recent usage, back-to-backs, pitches thrown, rest days, injuries, and likely availability. A season-long bullpen ERA alone is not enough.
Offensive Matchup Engine — Project each offense specifically against today's opposing pitcher rather than using generic team scoring averages. Include projected lineup, wRC+/quality metrics, handedness splits, strikeout tendencies, power, walks, baserunning, recent lineup changes, injuries, and hitter-vs-pitch-type compatibility.
Lineup Confirmation + Player Availability — Your projection should materially change when a star sits or when a lineup construction changes. Ideally you have a preliminary projection followed by an updated projection once lineups are confirmed. The model should know the difference between a normal lineup and a replacement-heavy lineup rather than treating the team as static.
Run Environment Engine — Baseball is unusually sensitive to external conditions. Weather, temperature, wind direction/speed, humidity, roof status, altitude, park run factor, HR factor, foul territory, and ideally umpire characteristics should modify expected run scoring. This is especially important for totals, run lines, F5, NRFI and YRFI.
True Probability / Fair Price Engine — Everything above ultimately needs to produce an actual probability. If TBM believes a team wins 58% of the time, its fair American price is roughly -138. Only after generating a model probability independently of the sportsbook price should you compare it with the market and calculate edge. Otherwise the model risks simply reproducing sportsbook expectations.
Market Intelligence + Sportsbook Comparison — A strong predictive model and a strong betting model are not quite the same thing. You need opening price, current price, movement, consensus line, sharp-book pricing, cross-book disagreement, best available odds, stale lines, reverse movement, and line-shopping value. A 55% prediction can be a great bet at +105 and a bad bet at -135. Price is part of the bet.
Bet Selection, Ranking + Price Guardrails — The model should not bet every positive edge. Establish minimum edge, confidence, market-quality and price requirements; penalize extreme favorites; prevent contradictory positions; rank correlated plays correctly; and distinguish Pass → Lean → Play → Elite Play. This is one of the biggest differences between a prediction engine and a long-term betting strategy.
Historical Tracking, CLV + Continuous Validation — Record the exact model prediction, edge, odds, sportsbook, timestamp, closing line and final result before the game begins. Then evaluate ROI, units, win rate, CLV, calibration and performance by market, edge bucket, confidence bucket, odds range, month and model version. CLV is especially valuable because wins/losses can be noisy; consistently beating the closing market is evidence your pricing process has information.
Risk Management + Model Discipline — Even an excellent model will have losing stretches. Use consistent unit sizing, maximum exposure per game/day, correlation controls, price limits and bankroll rules. Never increase stakes because a model is "due," never lower qualification thresholds because the board is weak, and never retrospectively change predictions. Long-term survival is as much about not making bad bets as finding good ones.
