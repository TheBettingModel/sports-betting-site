A. Historical database
The model should maintain multi-season history at the game, team, pitcher, hitter and market level.
Every historical MLB game should store at minimum:
Game information
* Date
* Season
* Game ID
* Away team
* Home team
* Venue
* Start time
* Day/night
* Doubleheader status
* Final score
* Extra innings
* Home/away result
* Run differential
Market history
* Opening ML
* Closing ML
* Opening run line
* Closing run line
* Opening total
* Closing total
* Individual sportsbook prices
* Consensus price
* Sharp-book price
You want this because eventually the model needs to know things such as:
“Historically, when my MLB model gives a road underdog a 4–6% edge at +120 to +160 with positive CLV, how has that profile performed?”

F. Lineup engine
This is essential.
Store:
* Projected lineup
* Confirmed lineup
* Batting order
* Player handedness
* Player offensive rating
* Injury status
* Rest status
* Replacement player
* Lineup strength
Calculate something like:
Expected Lineup Strength
based on all nine hitters and their likely plate-appearance share.
Then compare:
Projected lineup vs normal lineup
Examples:
* +4.2% stronger
* -8.1% weaker
Once lineups are confirmed, recalculate the game.

G. Batter-vs-pitch-type matchup
More useful than blindly using traditional BvP samples.
Compare:
Pitcher's arsenal
against
Opponent performance against those pitch types.
Example:
Pitcher:
* Slider 38%
* Fastball 35%
* Changeup 17%
Opponent:
* Excellent vs fastballs
* Poor vs sliders
* Average vs changeups
Generate a matchup adjustment.

L. Simulation engine
After player/team projections are created, run game simulations.
Ideally:
10,000–100,000 simulations/game
Output:
* Home win %
* Away win %
* Home -1.5 %
* Away +1.5 %
* Over probability
* Under probability
* F5 ML
* F5 spread
* F5 total
* NRFI
* YRFI
You don't necessarily need literal Monte Carlo for every implementation, but conceptually the model should produce full probability distributions rather than a single score.

M. Fair-price engine
This is critical.
Example:
Model:
Yankees win = 58.2%
Fair decimal:
1 / .582 = 1.718
Fair American:
approximately -139
Sportsbook:
-120
Now you have a potential pricing edge.
Calculate:
* Model probability
* Implied sportsbook probability
* Vig-free probability
* Edge
* Expected value

N. Remove sportsbook vig
Never compare the model blindly to raw implied probability.
For both sides, determine the book's overround and derive no-vig market probabilities.
Then calculate:
Model probability – market no-vig probability
This is a cleaner definition of edge.

S. Recommendation engine
Do not bet every positive edge.
Create progression:
Pass No meaningful advantage.
Watch Interesting but below threshold.
Lean Small validated edge.
Play Strong enough combination of probability + market + price.
Elite Rare, highest-quality setup.
Inputs should include:
* Edge
* Confidence
* Calibration
* Price
* Market quality
* Data quality
* Lineup confirmation
* Sharp signal
* CLV history
* Historical profile performance

T. Price guardrails
Very important.
Avoid automatically recommending something like:
Dodgers -360
just because win probability is high.
Your engine should evaluate expected value, not likelihood alone.

X. Calibration
This is one of the most important concepts in professional modeling.
If TBM labels 1,000 events:
60% probability
approximately 600 should win.
Analyze buckets:
* 50–52%
* 52–55%
* 55–58%
* 58–60%
* 60–65%
* 65–70%
* 70%+
If your 65% predictions win 55%, your probabilities are overconfident.
Correct the calibration.

Y. MLB self-learning engine
Your system should learn things such as:
Starter model slightly overvalues recent strikeout increases.
Bullpen fatigue signal provides additional predictive power.
Wind adjustment is too aggressive.
Road favorites between -150 and -200 are historically overestimated.
But learning must happen out of sample.
Never change weights simply because the last ten games lost.
